The Missing
Element

Every gadget you own is built from a few basic parts. In 1971 an engineer called Leon Chua predicted one more: a resistor with a memory. It would remember how much electricity had flowed through it, even after the power went off.

Fifty-five years later, chips that behave a lot like it exist. But the perfect version from Chua's equation has never been found, and scientists still argue about whether it can exist at all.

On the bench is one of those real chips, magnified. The glass block is a sliver of oxide between two metal strips. Watch the glowing thread inside grow and shrink, and look at the screen on the right. Its trace makes a bow tie, the signature of memory.

In 1971 Leon Chua proposed a fourth fundamental two-terminal element, the memristor, closing the symmetry between charge and flux linkage: f(q, φ) = 0. Real resistive-switching devices are now modelled very accurately. No device obeying the ideal law has been demonstrated.

On the bench: a crossbar RRAM cell (Pt / HfOx / TiN), simulated with a Stanford/PKU-type filament-gap model under a slow sinusoidal sweep. The scope shows its pinched hysteresis loop in the v–i plane.

Go deeper

What is a “circuit element”, anyway?

The simplest building blocks of electronics, each defined by one rule linking two electrical quantities. A resistor links voltage and current, a capacitor links charge and voltage, and an inductor links flux and current. Chua asked whether a fourth rule, linking charge and flux, deserved its own part.

A two-terminal element is defined by a constitutive relation between a pair of v, i, q, φ. R: f(v, i) = 0. C: f(q, v) = 0. L: f(φ, i) = 0. Memristor: f(q, φ) = 0.

Why would anyone want a resistor that remembers?

Three reasons. Memory chips that keep data with the power off and switch in nanoseconds. Brain-like chips, where a memristor plays the part of a synapse whose strength changes as it learns. And computing inside memory, which saves the energy normally spent shuttling data around. See “Computing inside memory” in the menu.

Non-volatile RRAM, analog in-memory matrix–vector multiplication in crossbars (Ohm + Kirchhoff), and neuromorphic synapses. Accuracy is limited by update nonlinearity, cycle-to-cycle noise, drift and endurance.

01 · Hands on

A part that remembers

Nobody has built a perfect memristor yet, so we sealed one in a black box and gave it exactly the behaviour Chua's equation describes. It's wired like a starter electronics kit: battery → button → the box → a resistor → two LEDs. Green lights when current flows one way, red the other way.

Picture the box as a water pipe that widens the more water has flowed through it one way, and narrows when water flows back. When the water stops, the pipe stays the width it was.

The black box is an ideal charge-controlled memristor, the component nobody has fabricated:

v = M(q)·i,  q = ∫i dt
M(q) = Ron + (Roff − Ron) / (1 + e(q−qc)/qw)

It sits in series with 220 Ω and anti-parallel LEDs (Vf ≈ 2 V), with Ron = 100 Ω and Roff = 10 kΩ. Holding the button integrates charge. The feedback runs lower M → more i → faster dq/dt, so the brightening accelerates. The real cell option swaps in a VTEAM-style threshold device: ẋ = 0 for |v| < vth.

New to breadboards? Switch on X-ray to see the hidden metal strips that connect the holes, then tap any part to learn it.

Try this

  1. Hold the button for a few seconds. The green LED starts dim and gets brighter.
  2. Let go, then tap it again. It's bright straight away, because the box remembered.
  3. Flip the direction and hold. Red glows and the box forgets.
  4. Swap in a plain resistor. The brightness is the same every time: no memory.
  5. Swap in the real cell, set the battery under 3 V and press. Nothing changes: a threshold.

The difference in the last step matters. The ideal box changes whenever any current flows, so even reading it disturbs it. Real cells have a write threshold, which is what makes them usable memories. It is also one of the things that make them non-ideal.

How a breadboard is wired inside

Under the plastic are springy metal strips. In each numbered column, the five holes a–e share one strip and f–j share another. The long rows along the edges (the power rails) are each one strip running the whole length. The groove down the middle separates the two halves. Why the LED needs a resistor

An LED barely conducts until about 2 V, then suddenly conducts a lot. Connected straight to a battery it would draw far too much current and burn out. The 220 Ω resistor keeps the current to a few milliamps.

With V_f ≈ 2 V and a 3 V supply, I ≈ (3 − 2)/(220 Ω + M). With the box fully ON (M = 100 Ω) that is about 3 mA.

Why the brightening speeds up

It’s a feedback loop. Current lowers the box’s resistance, lower resistance lets more current through, and more current lowers the resistance faster still. That’s why the LED creeps up slowly at first and then rushes to full brightness.

dq/dt = i = (V − V_f)/(R_s + M(q)) with dM/dq < 0, so the rate of change accelerates as q grows.

How the direction switch reverses the current

The slide switch has two sets of contacts that move together. With the two crossed wires (green and blue), sliding it swaps which end of the black box connects to + and which to −, so current flows through the box the other way.

A DPDT wired as a polarity reverser: the throws are cross-connected, so both outputs swap when the poles move.

02 · Chua's symmetry argument, 1971

Four quantities, six links

Electronics has four basic quantities: voltage (the push), current (the flow), charge (how much has flowed in total) and flux (voltage added up over time). Link them in pairs and you get six links. Five were already taken: two by definition, three by the resistor, capacitor and inductor.

One link, between charge and flux, had no part. It was like a gap in the periodic table. Chua predicted a part to fill it.

The four circuit variables v, i, q, φ give six pairwise relations. Two are definitions (i = dq/dt, v = dφ/dt). Three are the constitutive relations of R, C and L. Chua postulated the sixth, f(q, φ) = 0. Note that φ is flux linkage, ∫v dt, and not necessarily a magnetic field. That ambiguity drives much of the later debate.

Press and drag across the scope to drive the voltage by hand.

Go deeper

The “periodic table” idea, and its critics

Chemists once predicted missing elements from gaps in their table, and then found them. Chua’s square of four quantities works the same way, which made the prediction feel inevitable. Critics reply that two of the links are just definitions, not parts, so the “gap” may not be a real gap at all.

Abraham (2018) argues the 2008 device is not the 1971 postulate and neither is fundamental. Tellini et al. (2021) reach a similar conclusion within basic circuit theory.

What “flux” really means here

Flux linkage is just a running total of voltage over time. In a coil it equals the magnetic field threading the coil. For a memristor, Chua only needs the running total, but some physicists insist a true memristor must involve real magnetism, as an inductor does.

φ(t) = ∫v dt. In an inductor φ = NΦ_B. Chua’s definition needs no magnetic field, but critics argue the symmetry argument implies one.

Why each part draws a different shape

A resistor’s current rises and falls exactly with the voltage, so the trace is a line. A capacitor’s current peaks when the voltage is changing fastest, a quarter of a cycle ahead, which makes an oval. A memristor’s current is always zero at zero volts, but its resistance changes along the way, so it makes a bow tie.

03 · The ideal memristor

Charge writes the resistance

In the ideal memristor, the resistance depends on one thing only: how much charge has passed through, in total. Push charge one way and the resistance falls. Pull it back and it rises again.

It's like a footpath across a lawn. The more people walk it, the more worn and easier it gets. What matters is the total traffic, not when it happened.

The lower graph is the device's “rule book”: a curve whose steepness at the glowing dot is the current resistance.

φ = φ̂(q)
v = M(q) · i,  M(q) = dφ̂/dq

Any monotone constitutive curve φ̂(q) defines a charge-controlled memristor. Its slope at the operating point is the memristance. Because q = ∫i dt, M depends on the entire current history. The dual form is i = W(φ)·v with W = dq̂/dφ. The lower plot is φ̂(q), with the tangent at the operating point.

With a straight-line rule the steepness never changes, and the part is just an ordinary resistor. Memory needs a bendy rule.If M is constant, φ̂ is linear and the element is a resistor. A memristor is distinct only when it is nonlinear.

Press and drag across the scope to drive it yourself. Hold still on one side and watch the charge build up.

Go deeper

Memory with the power off

The ideal memristor’s state is the total charge that has passed through. With the power off no charge moves, so the state, and the resistance, simply stays put. That’s what “non-volatile” means.

At v = 0, i = 0, so dq/dt = 0 and M(q) is frozen.

Why the curve must bend

If the rule linking flux and charge were a straight line, its steepness (the resistance) would never change, and the part would just be a resistor. All the interesting behaviour comes from the bend.

M = dφ̂/dq is constant if and only if φ̂ is affine.

The twin version

You can also write the same idea the other way round, with the current controlled by the running total of voltage. It’s the same kind of part, seen from the other side.

Flux-controlled form: q = q̂(φ), i = W(φ)·v with W = dq̂/dφ (the memductance).

04 · “If it's pinched, it's a memristor” (Chua, 2014)

Three fingerprints

How do you spot a memristor? Plot voltage against current while you wiggle the voltage back and forth. Memory leaves three fingerprints:

Chua's 2014 operational definition says that any two-terminal device showing a pinched v–i loop under any periodic excitation is a memristor. The fingerprints:

  1. Pinched at the middle: zero volts always means zero current, so the loop crosses itself there.
  2. Loops thin out as you wiggle faster, beyond some critical frequency.
  3. A single line when the wiggle is very fast. The memory has no time to react.

Necessary, not sufficient. Lots of things with any kind of memory make a bow tie: a warm light bulb filament, for one. A bow tie shows that a part remembers something. It can't show that what it remembers is charge.Pershin and Di Ventra (2019) note that many systems with additional state variables pinch too. A pinched loop proves memory, not that charge alone is the state.

Go deeper

Why faster means thinner

Each half-wiggle pushes some charge through. Wiggle faster and each push is shorter, so less charge moves, the state barely changes, and the resistance stays almost constant. A part with constant resistance draws a straight line.

For sinusoidal drive the state excursion Δq ∝ A/ω, so the loop area → 0 as ω → ∞.

Things that make bow ties but aren’t memristors

An old-fashioned light bulb, a neon tube, even the ion channels in your nerves all draw pinched loops, because they all have some hidden memory (temperature, ionisation, gate states). That’s why a bow tie alone can’t prove a part is the perfect memristor.

Thermistors, discharge tubes and Hodgkin–Huxley channels are memristive systems. Pinched hysteresis is necessary but not sufficient for ideality.

05 · Strukov, Snider, Stewart, Williams, Nature 2008

“The missing memristor found”

In 2008, HP Labs announced they had found it. Their chip was a thin oxide layer with a conductive zone (amber) that grows like a rising tide as current flows. The more of the layer it fills, the lower the resistance.

The simple maths matched Chua's equation, and the world got excited. But a tide can't rise above the top of the layer, so modellers added a “brake” near the edges, called a window. Some brakes had a nasty side effect. Press the button below to see it.

TiO2 of thickness D as two series resistors: an oxygen-deficient region of width w and an undoped one. Linear ion drift:

v = [Ron·w/D + Roff·(1 − w/D)] · i
dw/dt = (μvRon/D) · i · F(x)

With F = 1 this integrates to M(q) ≈ Roff(1 − μvRonq/D²), which is Chua's ideal form, and the 1/D² scaling made it a nanoscale story. Windows keep x = w/D in [0, 1]: Joglekar 1 − (2x−1)2p, Biolek 1 − (x − stp(−i))2p, Prodromakis j(1 − [(x−½)² + ¾]p).

Why the HP model is now considered inadequate
  • Ion drift is not linear at ~108 V/m. Switching kinetics are exponential in voltage.
  • Conduction is set by an interface or tunnel barrier and, in many devices, by a localised filament, not a uniform moving boundary (Yang 2008, Pickett 2009).
  • There is no threshold, so any read current disturbs the state.
  • Window models are often ill-posed in SPICE and harmonic balance (Wang & Roychowdhury 2016).

Go deeper

Why “1/D²” made headlines

HP’s formula said the memory effect gets stronger the thinner the film: one over the thickness squared. Make the device ten times thinner and the effect is a hundred times bigger. That made it a perfect story for nanotechnology.

M(q) ≈ R_off(1 − μ_v R_on q/D²).

What turned out to be wrong

Real atoms don’t drift gently in proportion to the voltage. They hop over energy barriers, so switching gets exponentially faster with voltage. Most devices don’t move a smooth layer at all: they grow a thin filament. The simple model also has no threshold, so even reading the device would disturb it.

Nonlinear (sinh/exp) ion kinetics at ~10⁸ V/m; interface or tunnel-barrier conduction (Yang 2008, Pickett 2009); localised filaments; no threshold.

The Zeno problem

With some brakes, the tide gets halfway to the edge, then halfway again, and so on forever. On paper it never arrives, so switching would take infinite time. It’s like Zeno’s paradox of the runner who never reaches the finish line.

Mutlu & Kumru (2023): several window-function models have infinite switching time. In floating point the state instead snaps to F = 0 and locks.

06 · What the oxide is really doing

Real devices grow a filament

Zoom into a real memory chip and there's no smooth tide. Instead, a positive voltage tugs oxygen atoms (red) out of the glass. The holes they leave behind line up into a thin glowing thread, like a stalagmite growing upward.

When the thread almost touches the top, electrons can jump the last tiny gap and the resistance drops a lot. Reverse the voltage and the thread dissolves. The thread heats itself up as it carries current, and heat makes it grow faster still.

The best part is the threshold. A gentle voltage, used to read the cell, barely moves the thread. A slightly stronger one rebuilds it in nanoseconds. The lower graph shows how dramatic that is.

Filamentary VCM: field- and temperature-accelerated vacancy migration grows a conductive filament. The state is the tunnelling gap g. Stanford/PKU compact model:

I = I0 e−g/g0 sinh(V/V0)
dg/dt = −v0 e−Ea/kT sinh(γa0V / toxkT)
T = T0 + V·I·Rth

Thermally activated hopping over a field-lowered barrier (sinh·exp) and Joule self-heating produce extreme switching nonlinearity. SET is abrupt (positive feedback through T) and RESET gradual. Jülich's JART VCM instead uses Ndisc, the vacancy concentration modulating a Schottky barrier, with D2D/C2C variability and RTN. Bengel et al. (2021) validated it on Pt/HfO2/TiOx/Pt nano-crossbars.

Press and drag across the scope to set the voltage by hand. Creep up slowly and find the voltage where the thread suddenly snaps shut.

Go deeper

The very first switch: “forming”

A brand-new cell has no filament. Engineers apply a one-time, higher-voltage “forming” pulse to create the first one. After that, normal voltages grow and dissolve it.

Electroforming is included in later JART VCM versions and studied in 1T1R arrays.

Why heat is the accelerator

Atoms jiggle more when hot, so they hop over barriers much more often. Current heats the filament, which speeds up the hopping, which changes the current. That loop is why switching ON is sudden and switching OFF is gradual.

Rate ∝ exp(−E_a/kT)·sinh(γa₀V/t_ox kT), with T = T₀ + V·I·R_th: positive feedback on SET, negative on RESET.

Every switch is a little different

Atoms move randomly, so the filament grows a bit differently each time and differs from cell to cell. Turn on “random variation” to see the loop wobble. Chip designers have to plan for this.

Device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variability, plus random telegraph noise. Modelled with stochastic terms (JART v1b, Stanford/PKU variants) and 2025 stochastic compact models.

How real chips use these cells

Each memory cell is usually paired with a tiny transistor or “selector” that acts as a gate. It limits the current during switching and stops neighbouring cells interfering with each other.

1T1R and 1S1R array architectures; the compliance current sets the ON state.

07 · Chua & Kang 1976 · the framework that won

One recipe, many devices

Here's the device pulled apart. Every real memory chip follows the same simple recipe: a resistance controlled by a hidden dial. Current turns the dial, and the dial sets the resistance.

What the dial physically is changes from chip to chip: the length of a thread, how many atoms are missing, the thickness of a layer, how crystalline a material is, its temperature, which way a magnet points. Scientists agree on the recipe. The argument is only about whether the perfect version, with charge as the dial, exists.

v = R(x, i) · i
dx/dt = f(x, i)

A resistance controlled by slow internal states, with zero current at zero voltage (when R is finite). That minimal assumption is why the framework fits every device. It is also why critics call it a generic “nonlinear dynamic resistor”, since it covers thermistors, discharge tubes and Hodgkin–Huxley channels as well. It is a template, not a law.

The four classes in one sentence each

Ideal: resistance set by charge alone. Ideal generic: set by a hidden dial that is secretly just charge in disguise. Generic: a hidden dial that moves in more complicated ways. Extended: the resistance also depends directly on the current.

Chua (2015) classification: ideal ⊂ ideal generic ⊂ generic ⊂ extended.

Why critics call the recipe too broad

A recipe that fits everything from light bulbs to nerve cells doesn’t tell you much about any one of them. The physics, meaning what the dial is and how it moves, still has to come from studying each device.

08 · NbO2 · locally active memristors

A device that fires like a neuron

In this chip the hidden dial is simply temperature. Current warms it, and warm material conducts better, so more current flows and it warms further. Above about 800 °C it suddenly behaves like a metal.

Feed it through a resistor with a small capacitor alongside and it starts to blink. Charge builds up, the chip flashes hot, dumps the charge, cools, and goes again, much like a nerve cell firing. Turn the supply knob and find where the blinking starts and stops.

A thermal Mott memristor: state = temperature, with an Arrhenius insulator going metallic near Tc ≈ 1070 K. The quasi-static I–V is S-shaped, giving current-controlled negative differential resistance.

i = G(T) · v
Cth dT/dt = iv − (T − T0)/Rth

In a Pearson–Anson configuration, a load line through the NDR branch has no stable equilibrium, and the circuit relaxes into spiking. Kumar, Strachan and Williams (Nature 548, 318, 2017) found both transport-driven and Mott-driven NDR in NbO2 and drove it into chaos. Chua's local-activity and edge-of-chaos theory is the analysis tool.

Exciting for brain-like chips, but it's an active device, with energy pumped in to make it fire. It tells us nothing about the perfect, passive memristor.These are memristive systems, but locally active. They are irrelevant to the existence of an ideal passive memristor.

Go deeper

What a load line is

The rest of the circuit (the supply and the resistor) only allows certain combinations of voltage and current, and they fall on a straight line. Where that line crosses the device’s own curve is where the circuit wants to sit. If it crosses the backwards-bending part of the curve, there’s no comfortable place to sit, and the circuit oscillates.

Equilibria lie where the load line i = (V_s − v)/R_s meets the DC curve. An intersection on the NDR branch, with enough C, gives a relaxation oscillation.

The “edge of chaos”

Chua’s theory says lifelike behaviour (spikes, rhythms, chaos) appears in devices poised on a knife-edge between calm and unstable. NbO₂ cells can be tuned to sit right there.

Local activity theory gives a necessary condition for complexity. Kumar, Strachan & Williams (2017) used chaotic NbO₂ dynamics for optimisation.

09 · Pershin & Di Ventra, J. Phys. D 2018

The capacitor test

Here's a fair test of whether a part is a true memristor. Connect it to a capacitor, which is a little charge bucket. Every bit of charge that goes through the part ends up in the bucket. Push in a burst, then let the bucket empty back through the part.

At the end, exactly as much charge has gone back as went forward, so the books balance. A true memristor only cares about charge, so it must return to exactly where it started. If it doesn't, it isn't one.

Place the device in series with a capacitor. Then qM(t) − qM(0) = qC(t) − qC(0). For any excitation that returns qC to its initial value, an ideal memristor must satisfy Mfinal = Minitial. Here the drive ends at 0 V and the capacitor discharges fully through the device. Pass criterion: within ±10%.

Go deeper

Why one fail is enough

The rule says “a true memristor must always come back to where it started”. To show a part breaks the rule, you only need one case where it doesn’t. Passing a few tests can’t prove it, because the next test might fail.

Has anyone challenged the test?

Not in the papers reviewed here. The logic is simple and hasn’t been refuted. The honest caveat is that most published results come from one research group, so independent repeats on more kinds of devices would make the case stronger.

10 · Looking for the real one

The hunt for the perfect one

Some scientists think the perfect memristor needs real magnetism, the way an inductor does. So in 2019 a team threaded a wire through a magnetic ring and announced: “real memristor found”.

Try it yourself. Each pulse sends charge through the wire and then lets it drain back out. The charge always returns to zero, but the ring stays flipped. It remembers the direction of the last pulse, not the total charge, so it fails.

If φ must be physical magnetic flux, HP's TiO2 cell “would work in a world without magnetism” (Vongehr & Meng 2015). The 2019 “Φ memristor” used a wire threaded through a ferrite core. The toy model here: core magnetisation switches when the wire current exceeds a coercive value, while the series capacitor forces net charge to zero after each pulse.

A deeper objection: heat and noise

Some physicists argue that a perfect memristor would quietly turn random heat jiggles into usable energy, which the laws of thermodynamics forbid. If they're right, the perfect part can't exist. Others disagree.Sundqvist, Ferry & Kish (2017): the noise-free equations lack a fluctuation–dissipation theorem, so a passive memristor could rectify thermal noise in violation of the Second Law. Abraham (2018) calls the ideal memristor “an unphysical active device.” Circuit theorists reply that Wyatt–Chua passivity holds whenever M(q) > 0. The disagreement over the definition of passivity remains open.

Go deeper

Why look at magnetism?

An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field. If the memristor really is the “missing” partner in the symmetry, some argue it should involve magnetism too. HP’s oxide cell would work just as well in a world without magnetism, which is why critics say it can’t be Chua’s original part.

Vongehr & Meng (2015). Gale’s mem-con theory tried to give φ a literal magnetic meaning via Biot–Savart, but it needs fitted constants and hasn’t been adopted.

What would count as finding one?

A real, two-terminal, unpowered part that returns to exactly its starting resistance whenever its charge returns to zero, for any pattern of pushes, at ordinary conditions. Nothing has done that yet.

Pass the series-capacitor test for arbitrary excitations, be passive, and be driven by current rather than an external field.

11 · Where things stand, October 2026

Solved for engineers. Open for physics.

Settled

  • Real memory chips work like “resistance + hidden dial”, and we can simulate them very accurately.Real devices are memristive systems: several states, thermally activated and field-accelerated kinetics, strong self-heating.
  • HP's simple 2008 tide model is too simple for real chips.HP linear drift and simple window functions are inadequate.
  • A bow-tie loop alone doesn't prove a part is a perfect memristor.A pinched loop alone does not prove ideality.

Strongly supported

  • No one has built a perfect memristor.No fabricated device has been shown to be ideal.
  • Every real chip tested fails the capacitor test.Tested resistive-switching devices fail the capacitor test.

Still argued about

  • Whether a perfect memristor is physically possible.
  • Whether it deserves to be called a “fundamental” element.
  • Whether it needs real magnetism.
  • Whether the noise-free equations are thermodynamically consistent.

The bottom line. Engineers can design with these chips today, for memory and brain-like computing. Physicists still can't point to the perfect part from the 1971 equation, and many doubt it exists. Much of the fight is about names. If “memristor” means anything that leaves a bow tie, they're everywhere. If it means the perfect charge-only part, none has been found.

Bottom line. The problem is solved in the engineering sense and unsolved in the fundamental sense. State-variable compact models simulate real devices accurately. No device is known to obey v = M(q)·i. Chua's broad definition and the physicists' narrow one are each internally consistent. “The memristor exists” only has meaning once the definition is fixed.

If you need a model

  1. Choose by technology (station 07), not by name.
  2. Fit to your own pulse responses, not only DC sweeps.
  3. Include temperature and variability.
  4. Check that the model is well-posed (Wang & Roychowdhury 2016).
  5. To settle a claim of ideality, run the series-capacitor test.

Simulations use published functional forms with illustrative parameters, so they show behaviour, not any particular device's numbers. The 3D bench is ray-marched in a single shader. If you’re an engineer

Pick a model made for your technology, fit it to your own pulse measurements, include heat and randomness, and make sure it behaves well in your simulator. The word “memristor” doesn’t change any of that.

JART VCM for valence-change oxides, Stanford/PKU for HfOₓ, electrothermal models for Mott devices, VTEAM/Yakopcic for fast behavioural work. Check well-posedness.

If you’re a physicist

The open questions are whether a charge-only memristor can exist at all, whether it would have to be magnetic, and whether it can be made consistent with thermodynamics.

Physical realisability, the definition of passivity, fluctuation–dissipation consistency, and whether φ must be magnetic flux.

If you’re just curious

The chips are real and useful. Whether they are the mythical “fourth element” is a debate about definitions, and you now know enough to follow both sides.
